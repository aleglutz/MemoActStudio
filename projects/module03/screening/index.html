Module 03 — Ethics and Techniques of Intervening in Archival Footage

Everything screened today, in order, on one page. Thirty seconds of one archival film put through four rising degrees of intervention — quoted, sounded, sharpened, coloured — after the finished reel that all of it was made for.

The reel — Signed After Midnight

2:49 · 1080×1920 · twenty shots · Museum Berlin-Karlshorst

What the end of the workflow looks like. Three things worth naming after it plays, not before:

  • Every subtitle is verbatim from the script. Not one line was transcribed off the audio by a machine.
  • At 1:01 it uses the archival film below — the German delegation arriving at Tempelhof, from the same Karlshorst reel.
  • And it refuses to enlarge it. Cropping that shot to vertical would need a 2.4× blow-up, so it sits full width with bands instead. A decision from today's session is already in the finished thing — and the honest option was the one that looks less impressive.

Level 1 — quotes

30 s · 1068×800 · no model, no GPU

The document is the ground truth. A workflow decides when a line appears. It never decides what it says.

The three quotes are transcribed from the signed English copy of the Act of Military Surrender. Not from the footage, not from memory, not from a paraphrase. That rule is what separates this from the auto-subtitle button in any editor.

The stock graph can do the same thing visibly worse, and both limits are the argument for building a tool: one node holds one string over one frame range, so three captions are three runs; and its background colour draws no real plate, so only a drop shadow stands between white text and the picture. That second limit never shows on this film — the strip where the caption sits measures 14 to 65 out of 255 across the whole cut. Dark everywhere. A flaw your material happens to conceal is the expensive kind.

Discussion
  1. A caption is a verbatim quote from a real document. Does putting it over a different moment of the film make it a lie? Nothing in the words changed. Where does timing stop being editing and start being a claim?
  2. This reel already carries the museum's own burned-in captions. If yours contradicts theirs on the same frame, whose survives — and who decides?
  3. Does a quote belong on screen if you cannot also show where it came from?

Level 2 — sound

30 s · four generated layers · 6 s of GPU each · ≈3 cents

Ask before explaining anything: did that sound like a recording? Not one sample of it existed before it was generated. The film's own audio track measures −91 dB mean and max, end to end — every sample in one histogram bin. It is silent, not quiet. There was nothing to rescue.

Nobody captions a room tone. A generated hall murmur is received as atmosphere rather than as content, and it passes unchallenged where an invented image would be caught immediately.

Two of the four layers are second attempts, and the rewriting is the real craft. The first bed came back flat broadband hiss — a bad recording rather than a room — and the rewrite named what was in the room. The first press cameras came back a tonal hum, and the rewrite named the shape of the sound in time: spaced out with silence between them, no continuous tone. Both fixes were words, not seeds.

Nothing in the graph places a sound at a timecode. The model makes material; a person still decides when the shutter fires and whether the footsteps land on the man who is walking.

Discussion
  1. Nobody captions a room tone. Should they? What would an honest sound credit look like on a thirty-second video — and would anyone read it?
  2. The narration in the reel is a real recorded human voice. If it were synthetic, would you disclose that? Does your answer change for the crowd murmur? If it does, what is the difference you are pointing at?
  3. Stable Audio Open is trained largely on Freesound's CC0 library — sounds people recorded and gave away. So whose coughs are in that 1945 hall?

Level 3 — upscale

30 s · same patch of the signing table · plain enlargement left, restored right

Two things happen and only one is the one people expect. Edges get sharper — buttons, braid, the line of a cap. And the grain disappears, because to the model grain looks like noise.

The grain is the film. Replacing it with smooth cloth and clean skin is a decision about how the record should look. It is not a repair.

These faces are about twenty pixels tall in the original. No model can recover a face that small; it can only make one up. Which model you choose decides how much gets invented — measured on one face, plain lanczos adds nothing by definition, Remacri adds 3 %, and NMKD-Siax adds 41 % by painting speckled grain onto a forehead that has none.

And then the awkward part: the careful model is not available on Comfy Cloud. Two of the ones that are available are the two that document rejects.

Discussion
  1. The grain is the film. Is removing it restoration, or retouching? The same operation is called both, depending on who is selling it.
  2. Your platform offers two upscalers and both invent grain on skin that has none. You cannot install the careful one. Do you use what is there, or do you not upscale at all? Answer as if the deadline is tomorrow.
  3. Jackson's argument is legibility: at native size and speed, nobody watches, so nobody learns anything. Is that worth the invention? The reel above answered no — and lost part of its frame to bands for it.

Level 4 — colour

6 s · three panels — the film, the model's redraw, the restrained version

Asked to colourise, the model redrew. One frame came back as a different man: a new face, re-invented medals, no film grain, the room re-lit — a plausible modern photograph standing where a document had been. And there is no setting in between. At lower denoise the archival frame survives intact and almost no colour arrives; colour needs full denoise, and full denoise is what makes it redraw.

So the model is demoted. Keep the archival frame's luminance untouched, take only the colour from the generation, and no edge, no face and no grain can move — the shapes come from the film. It does not fix a colour that landed on the wrong object. Where the model put red on men's backs, the method faithfully preserves the wrong colour with crisper edges. Bleeding is fixable; misplacement is not.

Nothing enforces consistency between frames, so each frame picks its own palette. Measured against the film's own frame-to-frame noise as the floor, the tunic swings 4×, a face 4.6×, and a blank plaster wall 17.6×. The model is least stable exactly where it is most free.

Discussion
  1. The model returned a plausible modern photograph where a document had been, and reported success. Nothing failed. What in your process would have caught it? Be concrete: name the step.
  2. Keeping the film's luminance means no shape can move. Does that make the colour honest — or only make the invention harder to see?
  3. The blank wall flickers 17.6× the film's own noise. Is that a defect to fix — or the only visible sign the viewer gets that this is generated? Would you smooth it if you could?

Where would you have stopped?

  • 1. Added nothing to the image; took its words from a document.
  • 2. Manufactured something the film never carried — and nobody will question it.
  • 3. Invented texture inside the image, and removed the grain that was evidence.
  • 4. Invented the image.

No tool anywhere in this session said that a line had been crossed. Every one of them reported success.
